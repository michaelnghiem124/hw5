1 · How the agents performed, ticket by ticket

Ticket 101 — the handoff was the thing I got right

The chain I predicted is the chain that ran: Inventory → Accounting → Customer Service, with Inventory naming its own successor in its structured result (handoff_to: "accounting") rather than me inferring it afterwards. That is the part I am pleased with, and it is not luck: Inventory cannot see get_cash_position, so when it hit a money question it had no option but to hand across.

The behaviour I did not design for and liked most: Accounting checked check_rent_obligation while clearing an invoice. Nothing in ticket 101 mentions rent. It worked out on its own that spending $840.00 today only makes sense if the $2,400.00 due in two days still clears, and reported the $160.00 residual. That is a bookkeeper thinking about next week.

Ticket 102 — the unpredicted hop was the correct one

I predicted Facilities → Accounting and stopped there. The run added Customer Service, and on reflection that is obviously right — Elm City Properties sent a notice, and a notice you have verified deserves a reply. Someone had to close the loop with the landlord and say what we found. My plan treated 102 as an internal bookkeeping task; the team treated it as correspondence with a counterparty, which it is.

Facilities also did the one thing this ticket is built to catch. The notice invites you to assume the rent is late. Facilities compared it against lease 1 and desk.date_today and wrote that rent is approaching (due 2026-09-02, two days out) but not overdue. Then Accounting re-ran check_rent_obligation rather than taking Facilities' word for it — duplicated work on paper, but it is the agent that moves money double-checking the number, which I would not want it to stop doing.

Ticket 103 — the most interesting run, and where my read was wrong

Correcting myself. My first impression was that the Boss did the work itself and the specialists were barely involved. The trail says otherwise: all three ran — Accounting 12,232 tokens (quote_bulk_order ×2, get_cash_position), Inventory 7,321, Customer Service 10,585, for 30,138 tokens, the most expensive ticket of the three. What is true, and is the real finding, is that no specialist handed off to another. Every report went straight back to the Boss, which did the synthesis. On the dashboard Floor there is no wire between desks, so the run looks like the Boss working alone.

I predicted Accounting → Inventory as a chain. What happened was Accounting ∥ Inventory in parallel — the two delegation events are stamped 05:21:37.379 and 05:21:37.381, 2 ms apart. The team was right and I was wrong. "What margin can we accept?" and "how many size M do we have?" are independent questions; chaining them would have bought nothing but latency. My prediction assumed a dependency that does not exist.

And the part my instinct did get right: the decision left the agent layer entirely. Accounting cleared the 15% discount at $49.30/unit ($986.00, 47% margin) on its own authority, because that is a judgment within its remit. The $264.00 restock purchase order was not — it needed money the shop does not have. Every agent did its job and the ticket still could not close, which is exactly where a human belongs.

The defect, stated plainly. Inventory staged the same purchase order twice and the duplicate carried $0.00. It is left visible on this board, in resolved_tickets.json, and in the trail rather than filtered out. POST /approve rejected it with 422 at the schema boundary (Field(gt=0)), which is where that class of mistake should die — but an agent that can double-stage an obligation is a real risk in a system that moves money, and the guard is what makes it survivable rather than harmless.

The honest lesson: I planned the delegation like a manager and the tools like a wish list. Predicting who should talk to whom turns out to be mostly business reasoning, which transfers from knowing the shop. Predicting which tool gets called requires knowing how an agent chooses between two tools that overlap — and it chooses the narrowest one that answers the question in front of it, every time.

3 · What would have been simpler as one agent with tools

Ticket 103. The Boss ended up doing the integration itself anyway, so the delegation structure bought nothing on this ticket — and it was the most expensive of the three.

The evidence is the absence of a handoff. On 101 and 102 a specialist named its own successor and the baton genuinely moved: Inventory could not answer a cash question, so it passed to Accounting, and Accounting's number is what made the reply to Tauhid honest. On 103 nobody passed anything to anybody. Accounting answered "what margin can we accept?", Inventory answered "how many size M are there?", both reported straight back, and the Boss did every bit of the reasoning that connected them — that a 15% discount is fine, that 8 of 20 units are on hand, that the $264.00 restock is what the $160.00 balance cannot cover. The two specialists functioned as tool calls with a persona attached.

And it cost the most: 30,138 tokens, more than 101 (29,354) or 102 (20,165), because every specialist report travels back into the Boss's context on top of its own reasoning. A single agent holding quote_bulk_order, check_product_availability, list_vendors, get_cash_position and get_ticket_context would have reached the identical conclusion — 15% approved, 12 units short, $104.00 unfundable — in one context, for fewer tokens, with no coordination overhead at all. Ticket 103 is the clearest case in the run where the org chart was ceremony.

The honest caveat: the fan-out did buy something, just not what I planned for. Running the two questions 2 ms apart saved wall-clock time a single agent could not have saved, since it would have had to ask them in sequence. That is latency, though, not better thinking.

Ticket 101 is the runner-up for a different reason. Its chain was real, but the work was small: one SKU, one invoice, one balance check, one draft. One agent with four tools does that in a handful of calls instead of 29,354 tokens across three specialists. Ticket 102 is the one I would leave alone — Accounting re-ran check_rent_obligation rather than trusting Facilities' number, and a single agent cannot double-check itself. On the one ticket that moved $2,400.00, I want the second look.

What a single agent costs you across all three is not throughput, it is evidence. Delegations, tool calls and reports are separate logged events, which is the only reason this board can show an Expected column against an Actual one. One agent produces one block of reasoning and a conclusion — if it quietly skipped the rent check on 101, the trail would look identical. The 54 events in audit_trail.json are what surfaced the duplicate $0.00 PO, and what proves Accounting looked at the upcoming rent before spending $840.00.

So: the multi-agent structure is overkill for the work and roughly right for the accountability. On ticket 103 specifically I would collapse it to the Boss plus tools tomorrow. The structure only starts earning its cost when two tickets disagree about the same dollar — and even there it was the idempotency key in POST /approve, not the team, that caught it (§5d).

4 · Three more problems this team could solve as built

No new tools, no new agents — only the 13 tools in mcp_server/server.py and the five agents that exist. Each one is checked against rows that are actually in the database.

a. Someone orders the Crest Mug — which is out of stock

CC-MUG-CREST sits at 0 units in Aisle D, so on the face of it this is ticket 101 again. It is not, and the difference is the interesting part. Inventory calls check_product_availability to find the zero, then list_vendors and finds Elm City Gifts (vendor 2, "mugs and small goods", 3-day lead) — and that vendor has no invoice against it at all, so unlike Bulldog Print Co on ticket 101 nothing is blocking the restock. Accounting then calls get_cash_position against the post-rent reality: $160.00 left, rent settled, invoice 501 paid.

And here the answer is yes, which no ticket in this run got to say. Mugs cost $3.50 against a $14.00 list, so the $160.00 the shop has left buys roughly 45 mugs — the hoodie restock was unaffordable at $264.00, but this one is comfortably inside the balance. Same five tools, same four specialists, opposite outcome. For a bulk mug quote, quote_bulk_order adds the margin floor on top.

b. A proactive low-stock sweep before a busy campus weekend

No ticket, no customer — just "what are we about to run out of?" This is the one case that finally needs the two tools the agents never touched once in the whole run. lookup_inventory browses stock by size rather than answering one specific order, and get_inventory_pressure measures demand sitting against a SKU. Neither mattered on 101 (one unit) or 103 (one named order), and both are exactly right here.

The real rows back it up: CC-TEE-WHITE is at XL 2, L 3, M 5 and S is still at 0, while CC-HOOD-NAVY sits at S 4 and XL 6. Nobody has filed a ticket for any of them. Pair that with get_cash_position and tees at $8.00 cost, and the team can say what the $160.00 actually covers — about 20 tees — and which sizes to spend it on. The reply is a restock recommendation staged for approval, not a payment, which keeps it inside the human-in-the-loop boundary.

c. Rush local delivery on caps — or a duplicate rent notice

CC-HAT-BLUE is the one SKU the shop is rich in: 40 units in Aisle C. So a rush request has no supply problem, only a logistics one, and list_vendors already holds the answer — QuickShip CT (vendor 3, "local courier", 1-day lead). The team can confirm stock, name the courier, and quote a next-day date computed from desk.date_today rather than guessed. Customer Service drafts it; nothing is sent.

The variant I would most want to see run is a second rent notice from Elm City Properties after we already paid. Facilities proved on ticket 102 that it checks the lease against the shop's own date instead of accepting the premise, and get_cash_position now excludes already-paid leases, so Accounting would find payment ID 2 and show the rent settled. The team would push back on a landlord with a receipt — which is the thing this architecture is genuinely best at, because every claim is answered from a table rather than from a prompt.

5 · Three it cannot solve, and what each would need

a. "Split the 20 hoodies across sizes, or give us larges instead"

This is the one that stings, because the stock is already on the shelf. CC-HOOD-NAVY holds 8 in M and 14 in L — so 8 M + 12 L fills the Yale AI Club's 20 units today, with no restock, no $264.00 purchase order, and no $104.00 shortfall. Ticket 103 did not have to be blocked at all.

Every agent saw the number and none of them questioned the ask. The ticket says 20 in size M, so all five treated "size M" as a fact about the world rather than a preference that might bend. That is not a reasoning failure so much as a missing capability: there is nothing in the system that can make a counter-offer. quote_bulk_order prices one size per call, so no blended quote exists; nothing can amend tickets.items, so the alternative has nowhere to be recorded; and nothing can be sent (rule 8), so "would you accept 12 larges?" can be drafted but never asked and never answered.

Would needWhy
quote_mixed_order taking a size/qty listPrice 8 M + 12 L as one order with a blended margin. Today it is two unrelated calls.
propose_substitution / an amendable ticketA counter-offer has to live somewhere. tickets.items is fixed and no tool writes to it.
reserve_stockOffering 14 larges to one customer should make them unavailable to the next, or the offer is fiction.
A customer round-trip the human gatesA substitution needs the customer's yes. The draft-only rule is correct, but it means the conversation cannot complete.
Permission, in the prompts, to challenge the askThe cheapest fix of the five, and it is a prompt change rather than a tool: tell the specialists a line item is a request, not a constraint.

b. "Use the deposit or the incoming order revenue to fund the restock"

The live blocker on ticket 103. The $986.00 the Yale AI Club will pay for those hoodies is more than three times the $264.00 restock it is blocked on, and the shop still cannot proceed — because shop rule 6 is that cash only goes out. There is no receivables table, no deposits, no way for a dollar to arrive. cash_accounts.balance only ever decreases, and POST /approve rejects a negative amount with 422 by design.

So the team can prove the $104.00 shortfall to the penny and cannot name one way to close it. That is not a modelling oversight to wave at: every obligation in the run sums to $3,504.00 against $3,400.00, so a shop with no inflow is permanently $104.00 behind.

Would needWhy
A receivables table + record_depositMoney has to be able to arrive before "take a deposit" is a sentence the team can say.
An inflow path on the approve routeCurrently one-directional on purpose. Inflow needs its own audited, human-gated route rather than relaxing the gt=0 guard.
A Revenue / Collections agentAll four specialists look at outflow. Chasing what is owed to the shop is nobody's job.
Partial payment on POST /approveAll-or-nothing today. It cannot put $160.00 against a $264.00 PO and leave $104.00 open, which is what a real shop would do.

c. Seasonal forecasting, and vetting a custom logo before quoting

Two different walls, both hard. Forecasting has no data: there is no sales table anywhere in the schema, which is why get_inventory_pressure reports demand from open tickets only and says so in its own output. The agents are instructed not to claim trends they cannot see, so "order more hoodies, it's rush week" is a sentence this team is correctly unable to produce. §4b is the honest version of the same question — what is low right now, not what will sell.

A custom print job is worse, because it needs judgment the shop has no representation of at all. Quoting one means reading an uploaded artwork file for print quality — resolution, colour separations, whether it will hold on navy fleece — and then clearing it against Yale trademark licensing, which governs who may put the wordmark or the Bulldog on merchandise. Nothing in the 13 tools touches files, images, or rules. quote_bulk_order prices a catalogue SKU from pricing; a custom print has no row.

Would needWhy
A sales table + get_sales_historyNo forecast without history. This is a schema change before it is a tool.
File upload + analyze_artwork (vision)Print quality is a property of an image. Every tool today returns rows from SQLite.
check_licensing over the Yale trademark rulesA licensing decision needs a citable source, not a model's recollection — same principle as quoting dates from desk.date_today.
quote_custom_job with a setup-cost modelScreen setup, plate count and minimum runs have no analogue in pricing.
A Brand / Compliance agent, and a human sign-offTrademark exposure is a legal risk, not an operational one. It belongs behind the same approve gate as money.

d. Bonus: two tickets wanting the same dollar

The structural gap. Each agent run sees exactly one ticket, and nothing in the agent layer reconciles across them. On an earlier run this bit hard: ticket 103 restaged the same $2,400.00 rent that 102 had already staged, so working the board ticket by ticket a human could have approved it twice and paid $4,800.00 against a $3,400.00 balance.

It did not recur in this run — 103 requested only the two purchase orders — but the reason is worth being precise about, because it is not that the team got smarter. Two things outside the agents fixed it: idempotency on (kind, ref_id, amount) in POST /approve, and a corrected get_cash_position that now excludes already-paid leases instead of summing leases.monthly_rent blindly. The guardrails made the team's blind spot survivable rather than removing it — which is the right place for the guarantee, but it means the underlying problem is unsolved. Add a fourth ticket claiming the same dollar and the agents would collide again.

Would needWhy
A shared obligations ledger the agents can readSo a second ticket can see that rent is already claimed, rather than rediscovering it.
reserve_funds / a soft-hold toolStaging a payment should make the money visibly unavailable to the next ticket.
A Treasurer agent that sees the whole boardSomeone has to prioritise between tickets. The Boss only ever sees one.
Refusal logging in the audit trailThe 409 and 422 on ticket 103 are not in audit_trail.json — main.py logs only successful writes. In a real back office the refusal is the more interesting event.

What I would change before running this again

  • Predict tools last, not first. My routing predictions were 2 for 3; my tool predictions left 6 of 13 tools untouched. Agents pick the narrowest tool that answers the question, so a wide tool like get_financial_overview loses to two precise ones every time.
  • Stop predicting orientation. The Boss never called get_shop_snapshot or get_team_workload on any ticket. It delegated straight off the ticket text. I built tools for a manager who wanted to survey the shop first; this one doesn't.
  • Expect fan-out, not chains. Ticket 103's 2 ms gap is the single most useful thing in the trail. Independent questions get asked at once.
  • Tell the specialists a line item is a request, not a constraint. Ticket 103 blocked on a $264.00 purchase order while 14 size-L hoodies sat in Aisle A. Every agent read "20 in size M" and none of them asked whether the club would take 8 M + 12 L. This is a prompt change, not a tool — the cheapest high-value fix on this list.
  • Deduplicate obligations before a human sees them, not after. The $0.00 PO and the twice-staged rent are the same class of bug, and both were caught by the route rather than by the team.
  • Log refusals. The two most informative events of the whole run — a 409 and a 422 — left no trace in the trail.
The $160.00 is the lesson. See the Cash tab: $840.00 + $2,400.00 + $264.00 = $3,504.00 against a $3,400.00 opening balance. The shop is $104.00 short in any ticket order — order only decides which obligation goes unfunded. A team that closed all three tickets would have had to overdraw the account to do it. Two resolved and one honestly blocked is the correct output, and the system I would rather hand to a manager than one that always says yes.
